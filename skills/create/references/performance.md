## Brief

Build the smallest brief that can support a defensible ad hypothesis. Extract:

- business objective and desired viewer action;
- platform and placement;
- audience or segment;
- known pain, desire, objection, or use context;
- product/service and supplied proof;
- offer and exact customer-facing copy;
- available references and brand rules;
- constraints, required elements, and forbidden claims.

Do not require every field. An awareness creative may not need an offer. A service ad may not need a physical product. Ask only when the missing answer would change the central hypothesis or make the final visual unsafe or misleading.

Treat audience insight as one of:

- `evidence`: supplied research, reviews, interviews, analytics, or verified observations;
- `inference`: a reasoned interpretation of supplied facts;
- `hypothesis`: a testable assumption used because evidence is incomplete.

Never label an inference or hypothesis as research.

## Insight and angle

Express the customer insight as a tension the creative can address:

- what the person wants;
- what they fear or avoid;
- what feels inconvenient;
- what they have tried;
- what makes them hesitate;
- what would make the new path credible.

Choose one central angle. Common angles include convenience, value, relief, speed, trust, quality, status, simplicity, comparison, and transformation. Select the angle because it fits the objective, evidence, audience state, and offer—not because it is visually fashionable.

Keep one dominant promise or idea per creative. Supporting proof may reinforce it, but should not compete with it.

## Framework taxonomy

Choose one primary framework and optionally one supporting mechanism.

### Product

- `Hero Product`: make the product the unmistakable focal point.
- `Product + Benefit`: connect the product to one concrete benefit.
- `Product + Offer`: pair the product with a supplied, verified offer.
- `Product Demonstration`: show how the product is used or behaves.
- `Product Breakdown`: explain relevant components or features.

### Problem

- `Problem -> Solution`
- `Pain State`
- `Before / After`
- `Mistake -> Fix`

Use before/after only when the comparison is truthful, supportable, and appropriate for the category.

### Proof

- `Social Proof`
- `Review`
- `Rating`
- `Expert Proof`
- `Quantitative Proof`

Use proof only when the exact proof is supplied or verified. Never invent a review, rating, expert, credential, or number to complete a layout.

### Comparison

- `Them vs Us`
- `Old Way vs New Way`
- `Feature Comparison`

Keep comparisons factual and specific. Do not create competitor claims or logos without evidence and a legitimate need.

### Desire

- `Lifestyle`
- `Outcome`
- `Aspiration`
- `Identity`

### Native

- `UGC`
- `Smartphone Photo`
- `Creator Recommendation`

### Education

- `Ingredient Breakdown`
- `Feature Breakdown`
- `How It Works`
- `Reasons`
- `Myth / Reality`

### Offer

- `Discount`
- `Bundle`
- `Free Trial`
- `Bonus`
- `Limited Offer`

Use offer frameworks only with supplied terms. Do not generate artificial scarcity or urgency.

## Concept development

For a new campaign, propose 2-4 concepts that test genuinely different hypotheses. Use this compact format:

```text
Name:
Insight:
Angle:
Framework:
Hook:
Visual idea:
Why it could work:
```

Good differentiation changes the reason to pay attention or believe. Weak differentiation changes only background color, camera, decorative props, or superficial style.

Recommend a concept using explicit reasoning: match to audience state, product visibility, proof readiness, placement, and testing value. Do not claim it will improve CTR, CPA, ROAS, or sales before a controlled test.

## Product and UGC workflows

For a real product image, use the `product` operation and read `references-and-locks.md`. The product source is the only authority for geometry, proportions, packaging, label, colors, and materials. Never reconstruct packaging from memory when the source can be preserved.

For apps and services, replace product fidelity with message fidelity and a clear visual metaphor, interface treatment, human situation, or proof device. Do not fabricate UI, metrics, or customer results and present them as real.

For UGC, favor plausible native signals:

- smartphone photography;
- available or ordinary indoor light;
- slightly imperfect framing;
- natural skin texture and fabric folds;
- casual posture and believable object placement;
- an ordinary environment relevant to the use case.

Avoid beauty-retouched skin, plastic texture, perfect studio symmetry, generic stock poses, and unnecessary cinematic polish. Use concrete positive direction instead of a bloated negative prompt.

## Mobile-first formats

Support these primary placements:

- `4:5` feed;
- `9:16` Stories or Reels static;
- `1:1` square.

Design for a small screen:

- one clear focal point;
- readable headline with sufficient contrast;
- minimal competing text blocks;
- clear product or subject visibility;
- intentional negative space;
- hierarchy that survives thumbnail viewing.

For `9:16`, keep critical elements away from interface-heavy edges, but do not encode permanent safe-zone percentages unless the current platform guidance has been verified for the requested placement.

When adapting formats, recompose. Preserve the angle, copy, product identity, and approved brand rules while allowing spacing, scale, positions, negative space, and typography size to change.

## Exploration and exploitation

Use `exploration` when the goal is to find new hypotheses. Change the angle, framework, or central visual concept across variants.

Use `exploitation` when developing a winner. First identify:

- angle and hook;
- framework;
- composition;
- product treatment;
- proof element;
- environment;
- visual language.

Then propose single-variable mutations such as hook, headline, environment, product scale, human presence, proof, position, metaphor, lifestyle context, or composition. Lock everything else by default.

If performance data is supplied, use it to prioritize the next test while separating observation from causal explanation. CTR, CPA, or ROAS alone rarely proves why a creative worked.
